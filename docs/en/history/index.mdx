> **This volume's thesis**: Minecraft's success is not the story of "one indie game that sold well." It is the formation history of a medium that communities, platforms, and capital could redevelop again and again. What was sold was never content you finish and discard; it was a system you can inhabit.

## What this volume proves

Sales figures are easy to look up, but sales cannot answer the real question: why was it this game that a whole generation moved into, that mod authors rewrote, that server operators legislated, and that platforms and capital redeveloped over and over? This volume reads that history as the formation of a medium — not as a changelog, and not as a biography. You will see that every choice that looked accidental at the time — open development, a paid Alpha, no authored levels, a free hand for mods — later grew into the load-bearing structure of this medium.

## A map of the four parts

**Part One · Origins (2009–2011).** How a working prototype grew into a product on open development and a minimal loop: what Cave Game had to discard in order to exist, how Alpha's weekly updates turned players into co-authors, and why the Beta 1.8 controversy counts as the first design politics. Entrance: [From Infiniminer to Cave Game](/history/infiniminer).

**Part Two · Becoming a Culture (2011–2014).** How authors outside the base game — mods, servers, video — defined in reverse what Minecraft is. These three chapters are the historical premise of the next two volumes: Volume II on social design and Volume III on mod architecture both return here. Entrance: [Mods as the Second Author](/history/mods-as-authors).

**Part Three · After the Acquisition (2014–).** How capital, a two-product line, and version politics turned "protecting Minecraft" into long-term governance. What the $2.5 billion actually bought, why the two Minecrafts are not two skins, and how the official side and the community contest the right to define the game. Entrance: [The Microsoft Acquisition](/history/acquisition).

**Part Four · Dissecting the Business Model.** Revenue, the grey economy, competition, and legacy on one map: where the money comes from and where it cannot come from; why the most open ecosystem is the hardest to take a cut of; why "the next Minecraft" is hard to produce; and who preserves the worlds, the mods, and the servers. Entrance: [The Revenue Structure](/history/revenue).

## How to read this volume

Read in order if you like; the two shortcuts are marked here in advance. Design-minded readers can start at Part Two: chapters 4 and 5 (mods, servers) are the key to Volume II's social design and Volume III's mod architecture. Business-minded readers can jump straight to Part Four. What this volume deliberately does not do is also stated up front: no version-number listings, no server reviews, no YouTuber rankings — that is the Wiki's job and the list sites' job. This volume takes only the mechanisms behind them.
