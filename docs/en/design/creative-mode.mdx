> **This chapter's thesis**: Minecraft's four modes are four permission configurations on one world model, not four games. Creative mode still plays "like Minecraft" because it is parasitic on the block set, the scale, and the physics that Survival established; while that parasitism holds, infinite resources are not cheating — merely a change of trade. If you took Survival's blocks and physics out of Creative and players still felt they were playing Minecraft, this thesis would be wrong.

## The problem

Is a Creative-mode builder and a Survival-mode player playing the same game?

The list of differences between them can run long: the builder does not hunger, does not die, can fly, draws blocks from an infinite supply, and tears down an entire wall in a second. By the standard of most games, that is already two products. But put the two screens side by side: the same blocks, the same one block per meter, the same water that flows and sand that falls, the same holding left-click to mine and right-click to place — the builder has not opened some piece of "engineering software." They are still inside that world, only no longer governed by survival.

The real test is here: **did the world change?** If mode-to-mode swaps the world — different physics, different blocks, a different spatial language — then they are different games; if the world has not moved a hair and the only thing that changed is "what you are allowed to do to it," then they are permissions. This chapter argues that Minecraft is the latter, and draws up the permission table; then it answers a subtler question: what is it that infinite resources change? The answer does not live at the technical layer. It lives at the social one.

## Why Creative still feels like playing Minecraft

Check, one item at a time, what Creative mode failed to replace.

**The blocks were not replaced.** The several hundred blocks lying in the Creative inventory are, overwhelmingly, blocks diggable and craftable in a survival world — stone is survival's stone, glass is survival's glass; even the flower pot keeps survival's size. The builder's vocabulary and the survival player's are one and the same.

**The scale was not replaced.** The door is still one block wide and two tall, the player is still a 1.8-meter collision box, rooms still grow to the measure of the body. Creative mode provides no "enlarge tenfold" button; builds still look "like Minecraft builds" precisely because they are amplifications and combinations of a human habitable scale, not an abolition of scale.

**The physics were not replaced.** The easiest item to overlook: in Creative, water still flows, sand still falls, TNT still explodes (it just cannot kill you), and if you switch off flight you still fall — it just cannot kill you either. The world's equations are still the same equations; what changed is a handful of judgment switches that concern you (damage, hunger, item consumption).

**The verbs were not replaced.** Look, walk, jump, mine, place — the verb list from [The Minimal Verb Set and Game Feel](/design/verbs-feel) is untouched, right down to the crack animation while mining. Creative mode never handed the builder a new "drag out an entire wall" verb; blocks still go down one at a time — which is exactly why Minecraft builds, however grand, keep a handmade feel: it is the fingerprint the verbs leave.

Four checks done, the conclusion already stands: Creative mode changed permissions, not the world. The people who mistake it for a level editor usually put it the other way around — "no hunger, no death — does this still count as a game?" The question errs by taking survival's constraints for the definition of a game; chapter 3 ([Survival as a Meaning Machine](/design/survival)) made the point that survival is one meaning machine among others, and meaning does not come in only one kind. In Creative mode, the source of meaning has switched to a different machine: the work.

## Parasitic on Survival's rules

"Permissions don't change the world" is only the first half of the sentence. The second half matters more: **Creative mode's value comes precisely from its being parasitic on Survival's rules.**

The reason hides in a plain impression shared by the building community: you can recognize a Minecraft build at a glance. Why? Because the builder is building in survival's language — the block set is survival's block set, one block per meter is survival's one block per meter, and even the problems they wrestle with (cantilevers, spans, the fluid boundaries of water features) are problems set by survival physics. Where creation is constrained, the output speaks one dialect; the whole community's builds share one grammar, and that is what lets them be read against each other, compared, entered into contests, taught in tutorials.

Now run the thought experiment: suppose Creative mode shipped with a different rule set — custom grids of any size, smooth curved-surface tools, a material system answerable to nothing in survival. The builder's expressive power would certainly be greater, but the output would immediately stop looking "like Minecraft": it could no longer be translated back into survival players' worlds, the building community would split into sects of tools, and the identity of "built in Minecraft" would come apart. The experiment does not need to be run, because the control group exists in reality: creation tools that offer free-form surfaces and standalone editors do produce astonishing work, each in its own unmistakable style — and precisely such tools have never grown the "one language for everyone" prosperity of a Minecraft building community.

Among the four modes there is also a proof in reverse: **Adventure mode.** It is survival's mirror-image modification — the world's rules barely move, and what gets taken away is the player's right to mine and place. It is prepared for map makers: the author stages the script with commands and structure blocks, and the players who enter may interact but not rewrite the world. That Adventure exists shows that permission changes are a scale with two directions: Creative loosens upward, Adventure locks downward, and the world model remains the same throughout. And there is one read-only notch beyond it — Spectator mode: flight through walls, invisible, unable to interact, permissions shrunk to zero, a ghost of the world. From full rights to read-only, this is a complete permission spectrum, and every notch of the spectrum is still that same world.

## What infinite resources change: social identity

So what is it that infinite resources actually change? The answer: **not what you can do, but who you are.**

In survival, building is the sediment of labor: every block has a cost, and the house is a homestead. In creative, building is pure will: cost reduced to zero, and the output is everything. This rewrites the social meaning of building outright — it turns from a survival activity into a **publishing activity.** Around Creative mode the builder crowd grew a whole society parallel to the survival players': releasing and exhibiting works, juries for building contests, teaching-oriented "build tutorials," the division of trades on large collaborative builds (lead builder, terraformer, decorator). Under Survival, none of these practices can really happen — nobody rehearses an idea at the cost of thousands of hours of gathering.

So the sentence "creative is cheating" fails in a highly instructive way: cheating means **bypassing costs within one and the same evaluation system**, and the participants of Creative mode are not inside that evaluation system at all. Survival players compete in homesteads and staying alive; creative players compete in works and taste; the two currencies do not convert, just as a marathon champion and a synchronized-swimming champion cannot be ranked against each other. A survival player who switches into Creative, builds a city, and switches back has not "won" anything — unless the city is published as a work, or hauled back into survival as resources. The tool switches with the person, and identity switches with the tool.

The third identity is the one most easily forgotten: Creative mode is the **lobby.** Server spawn plazas, waiting areas, shop display halls are all built in Creative mode — because it is the cheapest way to dress a stage. Here Creative mode is neither survival nor artwork; it is facade. That a single permission configuration can carry three social roles at once — trade, stage, facade — is itself evidence of something: when the mode was originally designed, what the community received was an empty lot, not a manual of instructed uses.

## The permission table: four modes, one spectrum

Press the previous sections' conclusions into one table[^gamemode]. Watch the wording of each row: what changes is "the player's permissions over the world," never "what the world is."

| Mode | Life and hunger | Mining and placing | Items come from | Movement | Writes to the world | Typical identity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Survival | Can die, can hunger | Limited by tools and materials | Gathered from the world | On foot / vehicles | Yes, at a cost | Resident |
| Creative | Cannot die, cannot hunger | Unlimited, instant | Infinite | On foot + flight | Yes, at zero cost | Author |
| Adventure | Can die, can hunger | Blocked by default (the map grants exceptions) | Supplied by the map | On foot / vehicles | As the author defines | Visitor |
| Spectator | Untouchable, invisible | No | None | Flight through walls | None (read-only) | Ghost |

The table carries two footnotes of its own, each adding a layer of depth.

The first layer: **the mode axis and the difficulty axis are orthogonal.** "Easy / Normal / Hard / Peaceful" is difficulty; "Survival / Creative / Adventure / Spectator" is permission; the two switches are independent of each other — Creative on Hard difficulty is meaningless, and Survival on Peaceful is the Peaceful player from this chapter's opening. Beyond these sits a whole layer of finer knobs (game rules like keepInventory, the commands-and-cheats switch), which dilute the four notches into a continuous spectrum. The real permission model is far finer than "four modes" — chapter 12, [Who Makes the Rules](/design/who-makes-rules), unfolds this genealogy all the way to the server layer.

The second layer: **Hardcore mode is not in the table, because it is an extreme parameter of Survival** (death seals the world for good), not a fifth permission. Listing it beside the four modes is a common misreading in the player community; it belongs on the spectrum of death's restraint discussed in chapter 3 — restraint at one end, the one-shot at the other.

## Comparison: Studio, GMod, and the real level editor

Bring the other side back on stage (they were met in this volume's opening chapters), and this time look one level finer.

Roblox splits playing and making into two applications, and the making side (Studio) is a complete engine editor: free meshes, a scripting environment, asset management. GMod is the form in between: playing and making share one process, but making runs on an independent set of tool guns and menu systems, and the physics is ragdolls and hinges, not cells. What they share: **the vocabulary you build with and the vocabulary the world speaks are not one set** — things made in Studio enter the player's world only by being "published" as a game, and in GMod a contraption assembled with the tool gun and "the game's goals" are naturally two separate sheets.

Minecraft's Creative mode differs from them not in expressive power — judged as an instrument of fine expression it is the weakest of the three — but in **vocabulary identity**: the building verbs are survival's verbs, the building unit is the world's unit, building physics is world physics. So it needs no act of "publishing": what you build is already part of the world, and the next player who walks in is standing on your work.

And what is a true level editor? A tool "for designing an experience for others": the author stages a script, sets triggers, controls pacing, and the player consumes the design. By that definition, the closest thing to a level editor in Minecraft is, surprisingly, not Creative mode but **Adventure mode combined with command blocks** — that is the complete toolchain "for designing an experience for others" (its design implications unfold in [Commands, Datapacks, and Maps](/design/commands-datapacks)). Creative mode itself grew in exactly the opposite direction: not a tool for designing experiences for others, but **a workbench for moving yourself into the world.** A permission loosened out of survival ended by growing into a palette of social identities — the best possible footnote to "modes are permissions."

## What this chapter leaves behind

- **For players**: switching modes never once switched the game on you — what switched is who you are inside the same world: resident, author, visitor, or ghost.
- **For developers**: what you can take away is permissions orthogonal to content — make "what the player can do" an axis adjustable independently of "what the world is," modes become mere preset notches, and each community will grow the gameplay of every notch by itself. What you cannot take away is the parasitism: Creative mode's value depends on its being locked inside the same world model; loosen creation onto a rule set of its own and you get stronger tools — and lose the community's one universal language.
