> **This chapter's thesis**: the official standard for absorbing community work is **type**, not quality — things that add a verb (the piston), add misusable physics (riding the horse), or add a distribution format (datapacks) belong in vanilla; complete systems (industry, magic) and content packs stay with the community. If the official side absorbed a complete system and the mod ecosystem flourished because of it, this thesis would be wrong.

## The problem

Every Minecraft forum has one perennially pinned thread: "This mod is so well made — why not just add it straight into the game?" The attachment might be a screenshot of an industry mod, a graphics overhaul, or a modpack that rebuilds combat from the ground up. The subtext is a seemingly fair standard: **if it is good, it should be absorbed.**

The official side's fourteen years of absorption history (Volume I chapter 4, [Mods as the Second Author](/history/mods-as-authors), sorted out the facts: the piston went in; industry mods never did, not once) shows this is not the official standard. This chapter assembles the methodology the officials never said out loud — because to developers that methodology is worth more than the absorption history itself: it answers a general question. **When your product has a flourishing third-party ecosystem, what should you absorb from it, what should you leave in it, and how do you absorb without killing it?** That is also this chapter title's double meaning: mods are the community's design practice, and mods — as a phenomenon — in turn became the officials' design method: vanilla gets designed by deciding what to absorb.

## Where "why isn't this great mod in the game?" goes wrong

Start by taking the perennial thread apart. Its first mistake: treating quality as a sufficient condition for absorption. Quality is indeed necessary — the piston and the horse that entered vanilla were proven goods — but "good" means one thing in mod context and another in vanilla context. A mod's excellence often depends on its freedom: it serves only the players who chose it (an audience of enthusiasts by nature), answers to no hundreds of millions of newcomers, pays for no compatibility scenario it never picked, and can be abandoned when it breaks. The same thing, once in vanilla, carries the full burden of a decade of compatibility, onboarding, balance, and localization — **absorption is not an award ceremony; it is the nationalization of a work's maintenance costs.**

The second mistake: ignoring the crowd-out effect. The community's creative energy is not infinite, but its flow follows niches: whatever the officials make, the community gradually stops making — if the officials are going to do it, why waste the labor. Read in reverse, this is the officials' own position: **every region the officials leave blank preserves a plot of community land.** While industry and magic stay in community hands, they are the fifteen-year careers of tens of thousands of people; once taken over officially, careers become a few lines of changelog, and the relation between authors and players turns from partnership into feeding. Volume I chapter 4 said "not banning" is the cheapest governance; this chapter supplies the other half: **not taking over is the cheapest incentive.**

The third and slipperiest mistake: assuming vanilla and mods compete on the same track. Their actual division of labor was settled in Volume I — vanilla holds the minimal verb set at the size everyone can learn; mods push expressiveness to where enthusiasts will chase it. That division requires vanilla to **stay small.** Absorbing a pile of large systems means making vanilla do the mods' job — and once vanilla bloats, its own share (the part a newcomer can learn) is the part that drowns.

## Adding systems, adding content: two kinds of mods, two fates

Split the mod landscape along one axis: mods that **add systems** and mods that **add content**.

System mods touch the grammar: industry mods introduce "energy," a concept layer vanilla does not have; storage mods redefine "where items live"; rail mods turn transport into an engineering discipline. Their common trait: uninstall them and the gameplay built on top collapses a whole layer — the player has not "lost some things"; a whole class of things has become impossible.

Content mods touch the vocabulary: new blocks, new mobs, new dimension skins. Vocabulary creates fun too, but it hangs on the existing grammar — uninstall it and nothing collapses; there is just less garnish.

The distinction directly predicts two ecological fates. System mods **interlock with one another** — the power an industry mod generates drives a machinery mod's machines, a logistics mod's pipes connect the two sides' warehouses (chapter 1, [The Voxel as a Design Primitive](/design/voxel-primitive), covered this: a mod that inherits the primitive inherits the community's grammar for free) — and around them grow modpacks, tutorials, proprietary jargon: a civilization. Content mods mostly shine separately, unrelated to one another, and date quickly once updates stop. **Systems accumulate; content is perishable.** The rule also reads back at the officials as a hint about what to absorb: absorb content, and you get a pile of entries that will age; absorb verbs and rules, and you get grammar the community will keep mining.

## Modpacks: curation is design

On top of the interlocking of system mods grew a third layer of author (mentioned in Volume I chapter 4; this chapter unfolds its methodological meaning): **the modpack author writes not one line of mod code, yet performs game design of the most genuine kind.**

What is a modpack? Dozens of system mods, selected, ordered, trimmed, and reconciled: recipes that break balance get disabled, crafting paths that collide get rewritten, the advancement line gets redone with a datapack, and the whole pack gets a theme and a tempo — "grueling stone-age realism," or "an industrial farce where everyone has a jetpack." The finished product is a "new game" with players in the millions, and its design process barely touches Java: choosing mods is casting actors, changing recipes is rewriting the script, balancing is the director's craft.

This proves something the rest of this book needs: **design can happen entirely at the data layer.** Without touching the engine, without writing systems, merely by scheduling at the level of "what is allowed, what is banned, how expensive, how hard," radically different play experiences grow. The officials received this proof long ago and signed for it — the datapack system (chapter 8, [Commands, Datapacks, and Maps](/design/commands-datapacks)) lets every ordinary player do what modpack authors do, which is the official recognition of this layer of design practice. Curation is design — not a metaphor but a trade that feeds a real population in this ecosystem, a population you can count on the distribution platforms: CurseForge's Minecraft section passed two hundred thousand mods in March 2025, and Modrinth, the newer open platform, grew up alongside it.[^w-modding]

## Structural holes: the official restraint list

Volume I chapter 4 listed three structural holes: no energy (hence industry mods), crafting with no "discovery" (hence magic mods), a growth curve too short (hence RPG mods). This chapter renames them: **structural holes are the official side's restraint list** — every blank is one act, deliberate or lucky, of "the officials won't do it; let the ecosystem do it."

The beauty of the list is that it moves with time. Each time the officials absorb a verb (piston, horse, command block), the ecosystem clears new ground (piston-door aesthetics, horse-ranch gameplay, command-block maps); each time the officials build a system of their own, a hole may get filled — and a career crowded out. Across fifteen years the list has on the whole expanded, and not because the officials did nothing, but because they held to the previous chapter's legislative layering ([Who Makes the Rules](/design/who-makes-rules)): **the physics layer official, the gameplay layer communal, with the officials only ferrying verbs between the two.** The hole list has therefore become a map of community industry — a career of tens of thousands of people inside every hole. It is the kind of map only someone who treats the ecosystem as a partner rather than an outsourcing vendor would dare to draw.

## Absorption principles and counter-examples

Now promote Volume I's history into executable criteria. **The four principles of absorption:**

1. **Absorb verbs; let systems go.** What gets absorbed should be "new actions players can take away and combine" (push and pull, riding and taming, command execution), not self-contained complete gameplay (an energy economy, a spell system). The former hands the whole community a lever; the latter hands the whole community a script.
2. **Absorb formats; let content go.** Containers for distribution and extension — datapacks, resource packs — deserve officialization: they make creation easier for everyone and compete with no one. The reverse — absorbing one specific mod's specific content (a set of equipment, one biome's lore) — means the officials pulling up a chair at their own community's table. The officials once tried to absorb distribution itself: the official mod repository announced in 2012 never shipped[^w-modding] — while the formats, datapacks above all, succeeded. Formats officialize well; marketplaces belong to the community.
3. **Absorb only what the wild has validated for years.** The piston was validated in Infiniminer; the horse in Mo' Creatures[^piston] — before entering vanilla, both had run in the community for years, their pleasures and their costs on public record. The officials should not use absorption to do the community's trial-and-error, only to regularize what survived it.
4. **Absorb while preserving the niche.** The piston's author was written into the credits; the horse's author assisted the adaptation (Volume I chapter 4) — the posture of absorption decides the ecosystem's response: credit and collaboration let the community see that "regularization is an honor"; silent annexation teaches the next author never to open-source again.

**Four counter-examples (the classic postures of violating the principles):**

1. **Absorbing by quality.** "Tens of millions of downloads — this should be in the game." The highest-quality mods tend to be the deepest systems (the top-tier graphics and performance solutions, for instance); absorbing one means the officials taking over a project they may not be able to feed, while announcing to every author in the genre that the ceiling has arrived. The real contrast runs the other way: for performance and graphics enhancements the officials chose **in-house absorption** — slowly building community-validated directions into the versions with their own implementations, while the original mods keep existing. A reminder that "absorb" has more than one posture.
2. **Absorbing content by popularity.** "This creature mod is the hottest — add it." Content absorption is a one-time trade that buys out an entry; heat migrates, and the ecosystem's affections hold grudges.
3. **Doing the community's trial-and-error.** Moving a system that has just sprouted — unvalidated by any time — straight into vanilla: if it is wrong, the wrongness now belongs to the officials, and it will not get fixed for a decade.
4. **Silent annexation.** Moving a community work's approach into vanilla with no credit, no collaboration, no explanation — cheaper in the short run; in the long run, half the door to the authors' seats swings shut.

One exam question still has to be handled — the easily confused case from the memos: **runtime editing tools** (the selection tools of the WorldEdit kind, client-side building editors). Do they "add a system"? In this chapter's framework they are **verbs specialized into trades** — professional instruments serving a particular identity (builders, mapmakers), not new permissions over the world. Chapter 4's conclusion ([Creative Mode Is Not a Level Editor](/design/creative-mode)) gets reused here: a tool can be powerful, but it cannot displace creation as the residence permit of the world. Bedrock Edition has in recent years been building an official editor of its own, and its official positioning is the same: "a tool, not a game mode." The prosperity of the tool layer answers a real need of operators and creators (live multiplayer previews, collaboration on building sites), and its existence does not contradict Creative mode's positioning. The absorption principles apply to such cases as follows: absorb the "formats and interfaces" (the data formats of selections and blueprints), let the "specific tools" go, and leave the trade tools in the hands of the community that iterates fastest.

## What this chapter leaves behind

- **For players**: that your favorite mods never "went into the game" is not an injustice — only by staying in the community could they grow into an ecosystem, bind into modpacks, and raise up authors. Only the piston and the horse entered vanilla, and behind them stands the entire world that did not.
- **For developers**: what you can take away is the four principles of absorption — verbs in, systems stay; formats in, content stays; regularize only what time has validated; preserve the author's seat when you absorb. What you cannot take away is the confidence to leave blank space: the officials dared to leave industry and magic — the largest populations of all — outside the door because they were certain their vanilla stands without them. If your product is not sure what it stands on, it will not dare leave anything blank.
